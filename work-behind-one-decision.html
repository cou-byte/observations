What this site is

During 2026–27 the University of Florida Lastinger Center for Learning is traveling to ten regions of Florida to listen to teachers, school and district leaders, data staff, families, and community partners talk about one thing: how they use education data, where it helps, and where it gets in the way. This site is where we share what we hear.

What this page is

One story from that listening. It follows a single, ordinary decision in a school and shows the work it took to get there. It is about three minutes to read.

This story is illustrative. The people and details are a stand-in until a reviewed participant account, verified against the recording, takes their place.
A story from the Listening Tour

The work behind one decision.

A school wants to know which third graders should get extra reading help before the winter benchmark. Every number it needs already exists. Here is what happened between "the data are there" and "we decided."

Setting an elementary schoolTime span about two weeksPeople involved six
Day 1

Two weeks, fifteen seats, and a list that doesn't exist yet.

The school runs small reading groups after the fall benchmark. There are fifteen seats, and groups start in two weeks. The question for the reading coach is simple to say: which third graders should be in them?

The answer is somewhere in the building. Benchmark scores, attendance, last year's interventions, what each teacher has seen since August. None of it is in one place, and none of it is in a form that answers the question.

Day 1, afternoon

Four places, four schedules.

The benchmark scores are in the assessment vendor's portal. Attendance and enrollment are in the student information system, which the coach can view one student at a time but cannot export. Last year's intervention history is a spreadsheet the previous coach built and shared before leaving. What teachers have noticed this fall is in their notebooks and in email.

Knowing which system holds which piece, and who can log in to each, is its own kind of expertise. In this school, two people have it.

The hidden workBefore any data are touched, someone has to know where they live.
Days 2 to 4

The wait.

The assessment portal exports a file in a few clicks. The student information system needs district-level access, so the coach emails the district data specialist with a request. It is a two-minute query. It runs on day four, because it sat behind nineteen other requests from other schools, each phrased a little differently, each urgent to someone.

The intervention spreadsheet arrives by email the same afternoon. Three files, three layouts, and no shared student ID across all three.

The hidden workThe wait is not the query. It is the queue the query sits in.
Day 4, evening

Same students, different names.

In one file the student ID has a leading zero; in another it doesn't. One file uses legal first names; the spreadsheet uses the names teachers actually say. The coach starts matching rows by hand at the kitchen table.

Halfway through, the data specialist sends a crosswalk of student IDs she had built last spring for an unrelated request from another school. It finishes the job in twenty minutes. Nobody had asked for it because nobody knew it existed.

The hidden workThe matching lives in a personal spreadsheet. If the coach leaves, the method leaves with her.
Day 5

A low score with fourteen absences is a different question.

The combined sheet flags twenty-two students below the cut score. The coach takes the list to three third-grade teachers with one specific question: for each name, is there something the sheet can't see?

It takes one planning period. Four students missed most of the benchmark window. Two are already in a group. One had a hard month at home and is already climbing back. The list of twenty-two becomes fifteen who need something new, and the teachers are now part of the decision rather than recipients of it.

The hidden workAsking a specific question about named students took minutes. A general request for "input" would have taken a meeting.
Days 6 to 14

The decision, and the part that repeats.

The principal approves three groups of five. She trusts the list because she knows who built it and could ask them how. A letter goes home to fifteen families: what the school saw, and what will change on Monday.

The decision took one meeting. The preparation took about two weeks of five people's partial time. After the winter benchmark, the school will need the list again, and every step above will happen again, unless something about the systems, or the people, changes.

Why this story is herePeople across Florida told us versions of this. The data are everywhere. Useful data take work, and most of that work is invisible to the people who rely on its results.

What it can't tell you

One story is one story.

A single account shows one workflow, in one school, at one time. It cannot say how common that workflow is, whether it looks different in a small district or a large one, or whether the fixes that would help here would help elsewhere. Those are questions for the analysis across all ten regions, and we will label what we find as emerging until it is validated.

Where you see a proposal for change on this site, it will say whose proposal it is: a participant's, or the Center's.
